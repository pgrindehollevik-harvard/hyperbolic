\begin{abstract}
Hyperbolic geometry is theoretically better suited than Euclidean
geometry to representing tree-like data: the volume of a ball at
radius $r$ grows exponentially in the Poincar\'e model and only
polynomially in $\mathbb{R}^d$, matching the way a tree's leaf count
grows with depth. We test whether this mathematical advantage
translates into a practical one for representing the hierarchy of
artistic styles. Using frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 features over 81{,}446
WikiArt paintings spanning 27 styles, we train two prototype
classifiers that are identical apart from the geometry of their
output space ($\mathbb{R}^d$ vs.\ Poincar\'e ball of dimension $d$),
sweeping embedding dimension, curvature, and the strength $\lambda$
of a hierarchy-aware regularizer. Across 150 seed-replicated runs the local advantage of hyperbolic
embeddings is real, robust, and growing with embedding dimension:
hyperbolic prototype embeddings recover sibling and cousin
neighborhoods 4--5 percentage points more faithfully (recall@5) than
matched Euclidean embeddings, the gap widens with $d$, the
hierarchy-aware loss does not erase it, and it is independent of
which of three reference trees we use. The picture for global tree
fidelity is more delicate. Against the hand-built reference tree,
Euclidean prototypes correlate slightly better than hyperbolic ones
with tree distances; but the hand-built tree correlates only weakly
($\rho = 0.08$) with a data-driven empirical tree built from
training-set CLIP centroids, and against that empirical tree the
global advantage flips: hyperbolic prototypes align more closely at
every $d \geq 4$. The cross-entropy classification gap remains in
Euclidean's favour. We recommend treating geometry choice as a
clear local advantage for hyperbolic embeddings plus a clear
classification-accuracy advantage for Euclidean embeddings, while
remaining cautious about global-tree-fidelity claims that depend on
a specific hand-built reference.
\end{abstract}
