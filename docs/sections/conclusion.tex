\section{Conclusions \& Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Hyperbolic geometry improves a specific kind of tree-aware behaviour
--- the local fidelity of an embedding's nearest-neighbor structure
--- but does not improve classification accuracy or global tree
fidelity. Across our sweep, the geometry choice acts as a parameter
governing a clear trade-off rather than a hyperparameter to be tuned
toward a single optimum. The Poincar\'e ball is the appropriate
choice for downstream tasks dominated by retrieval inside fine-grained
taxonomies; Euclidean embeddings remain preferable for tasks whose
primary metric is per-image classification accuracy or coarse global
hierarchy structure. The two objectives are in measurable tension,
and the hierarchy-aware regularizer we tested does not resolve it.

These results do not establish that hyperbolic embeddings are
universally preferable for hierarchical image data; on global metrics
the opposite holds in our sweep. They do not establish that the
hand-built lineage tree is the correct reference, since tree-Spearman
and dendrogram F1 are by construction anchored to that specific
taxonomy. They do not generalize beyond medium-scale,
Western-canon-heavy WikiArt with a frozen CLIP backbone; a different
dataset, a fine-tuned encoder, or a substantially larger embedding
might change the picture.

Three limitations point to natural extensions. First, only the
prototypes live on the manifold; the head MLP remains Euclidean, so
the hyperbolic geometry shapes the decision boundary but not the
representation itself. A fully hyperbolic head along the lines of
M\"obius layers \citep{ganea2018hyperbolic} would extend the
geometry's reach and may shift the trade-off. Second, the regularizer
is a single fixed loss: scale-invariant MSE on pairwise distances.
Alternatives that match the rank order of tree distances (a
Spearman-style surrogate) or that weight nearby pairs more heavily
(Sammon-style) may achieve comparable tree fidelity at smaller
classification cost. Third, the reference tree is fixed by hand;
learning the tree from data along the lines of \citet{chami2020trees}
would test whether the hand-built hierarchy is itself a bottleneck
for the global metrics, and in particular whether the local-vs-global
gap is partly an artifact of imposing a tree that the data does not
fully support.
